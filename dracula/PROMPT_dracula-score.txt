PROMPT_dracula-score.txt   —   AMENTI STUDIOS · DRACULA · THE SCORE
==============================================================================

AI first pass. Four cues. Light piano and strings throughout; horns reserved.

WHY FOUR AND NOT TWENTY-SEVEN. The book is a dossier and the score follows the
DOCUMENT, not the scene. Seward's diary sounds the same whether he is doing a
transfusion or opening a tomb, because the diary is the frame the listener is
inside. Scoring per scene would be twenty-seven cues and would also tell the
listener what to feel, which the text does better.

THE INSTRUMENT RULE, AND IT IS THE WHOLE IDENTITY.
  PIANO AND STRINGS carry everything. Sparse, low, unhurried.
  HORNS APPEAR TWICE IN THE ENTIRE PRODUCTION — the pursuit cue, and once
  inside the Demeter. That is the point of reserving them. A horn that is
  always there is an orchestra; a horn that arrives in chapter 26 is an event.

WRITE LONG AND SPARSE, NOT SHORT AND LOOPED. A forty-minute chapter over a
two-minute loop is heard as a loop by the fourth pass. Each cue is generated
LONG — four to six minutes — and laid under the reading at the prime lengths
the sound sheet uses, so score and atmosphere are one mechanism and never
realign. Ask for a piece that goes almost nowhere; that is a feature here.

UNDER SPEECH, ALWAYS. These sit beneath a voice at low gain. Anything with a
strong melody in the 200-900 Hz range will fight the reading. Keep the
register low and leave the middle empty.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

### 1 · DREAD           Harker's journal. Chapters 1-4, 19, 22.

Slow sparse solo piano with low sustained strings, minor key, extremely
restrained, single notes with long silences between them, cellos and basses
holding beneath, no melody, no resolution, no percussion, no crescendo, very
quiet and patient, almost static, cold and spacious, ambient classical,
6 minutes

AVOID  horns, drums, choir, organ, harpsichord, any theme that resolves,
       anything that announces menace. The text is already frightening.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

### 2 · PASTORAL TURNING   The Whitby chapters. 6-8, 11-13.

Warm open piano with soft high strings, major key at first and souring
gradually without changing key, gentle and unhurried, a sense of English
coast and summer going wrong, strings thinning as it proceeds, no percussion,
no brass, quiet and breathing, ambient classical, 6 minutes

AVOID  a clear turn to minor. The whole idea is that nothing changes and it
       gets worse anyway. No horns. Nothing nautical or folk.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

### 3 · THE PHONOGRAPH      Seward's diary. 9-10, 14-18, 21, 23-25.
                            LO-FI. AND IT IS NOT A STYLE CHOICE.

SEWARD KEEPS HIS DIARY ON A PHONOGRAPH. Chapter 24 is headed phonograph diary
outright, and in chapter 17 Mina transcribes the cylinders onto a typewriter.
So wax-cylinder noise under Seward is not retro dressing — it is what the
reader is literally hearing. Lo-fi as a genre is built from exactly that
material: crackle, a slow break, a sampled piano. The lineage lands where the
text already is.

Slow lo-fi break at 70 BPM, soft dusty kick and a brushed off-beat, heavy
vinyl crackle and wax surface noise throughout, a simple sampled piano figure
slightly detuned and slightly behind the beat, warm low bass, no bright
treble, nothing above 4 kHz, hazy and narrow like an old recording, no
melody in the vocal range, 6 minutes

AVOID  hi-hats in sixteenths, anything crisp, any modern snap. It is wax, not
       tape. No build, no drop. And nothing lush — he is a doctor dictating
       at the end of a long day.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

SIXTEEN OF TWENTY-SEVEN CHAPTERS ARE THIS DOCUMENT, SO IT IS TIERED.
A breakbeat under eleven chapters becomes wallpaper, and wallpaper is worse
than nothing. Three levels:

  FULL      beat, crackle, piano.      ch 9, 10, 14, 15, 17, 18, 23, 25
            The procedural chapters, where the recording quality IS the
            character — a man filing observations about a case he is losing.

  CRACKLE   surface noise only. No beat, no piano.     ch 16, 21, 24
            Chapter 16 is the stake. Chapter 21 is Renfield on the floor and
            what they find in Mina's room. Chapter 24 is the hypnosis. The
            document is still a cylinder, so the noise stays — but a beat
            under any of those three would be obscene.

  NONE      silence.        ch 12 — Lucy's death
            The one chapter in the production with no score of any kind. Not
            a reduced cue. Nothing.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

### 3b · THE MEDIUM DOCTRINE        every document, not only Seward

THE DOSSIER SHOULD BE AUDIBLE. Stoker names the document type at the head of
every chapter, and the production already casts a voice per document. Give
each one its MEDIUM as well and the listener always knows which document they
are inside without being told.

  phonograph      Seward         wax: narrow band, surface noise, slight wow
  typewriter      Mina           clean, close, no artefact
  handwritten     Harker, Lucy   clean, roomy, private
  print           the cuttings   flat and formal, no room at all
  ship's log      the Demeter    clean, but the bed does the work

Nobody will articulate why Seward sounds different. They will simply know.

THE BEST MOMENT THIS BUYS IS CHAPTER 17, where Mina transcribes the cylinders
onto the typewriter. The treatment can CHANGE MID-CHAPTER — phonograph while
she listens, clean as she types — and the chapter is about exactly that act.
The form explains itself and nobody has to say so.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

THE PHONOGRAPH FILTER IS DONE LIVE, NOT RE-RENDERED.

The voice is already on a buffer source in Web Audio, so the cylinder
treatment is a chain on the node and costs nothing:

    bandpass   200 Hz - 3 kHz, Q around 0.7
    noise      a low-level looping buffer under it, -28 dB or so
    wobble     detune oscillating a few cents at ~0.3 Hz, for wax wow
    output     into the voiceGain with everything else

NO RE-RENDER AND NO NEW CACHE KEYS. The key is TTS_MODEL + voice + style +
text, and this touches none of them — it is applied after the buffer comes
back. Re-rendering Seward's part to bake in crackle would mean paying for
sixteen chapters of audio twice and could never be undone.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

### 4 · PURSUIT         The chase east. Chapters 26-27.    ← HORNS HERE

Driving low strings with a steady pulse, piano in octaves, and FRENCH HORNS
entering over the top — the only brass in the production — building and
insistent, cold and open like a snow landscape, forward motion throughout,
minor key, no resolution at the end, orchestral but restrained, 5 minutes

THE HORNS ARE THE WHOLE REASON THIS CUE EXISTS. Three hundred pages of piano
and strings make a horn in chapter 26 land like a door opening. Do not spend
them anywhere else.

AVOID  drums, choir, anything triumphant. They are chasing a box on a cart
       through snow before sundown. It is grim, not heroic.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

### 5 · THE EARTH-BOXES        ch 19-20.    INDUSTRIAL. 60 BPM HALFTIME.

FIFTY CRATES OF TRANSYLVANIAN SOIL, MOVED AROUND LONDON AGAINST A CLOCK.
This is the only genuinely kinetic, procedural sequence in the book — men
doing hard physical work in warehouses and carts, racing a sunset. It was
scored clinical in the first pass and that was wrong. It is the most moving
thing in the novel and it should drive.

THE INDUSTRIAL SOUNDS ARE THE PERCUSSION. Not layered over a drum kit —
THEY ARE THE KIT. A crate landing on stone is the kick. A chain pulled
through an iron ring is the hat. A lid levered off is the snare. Everything
is a real object from the scene, pitched and placed on the grid.

Two reasons this is the right build and not just a nice idea. It is diegetic
— the listener hears men working and only gradually notices it is in time.
And it clears: every hit is a field recording or CC0, so there is no borrowed
break in the one part of the production that would otherwise need clearing.

60 BPM HALFTIME, WHICH IS NOT THE SAME AS SLOW. Kick on 1, heavy hit on 3,
and the top end moving at double that. Written as 60 it reads hard; written
as 120 it reads busy. The brief must say halftime or the generator returns
something sluggish.

Hard driving industrial halftime at 60 BPM, built from sampled warehouse
sounds — heavy wooden crates landing on stone as the kick, chain through
iron as the hi-hat, a crate lid levered off as the snare, rope, cartwheels
on cobbles, a far-off dock crane. Low sub bass under it. One cold detuned
piano note repeating, nothing more than that. Relentless, no variation in
the pattern, no fills, no build, no drop. It should sound like labour, not
like a track. 5 minutes

AVOID  a melody of any kind, cymbals, anything triumphant, anything that
       sounds like a montage. These men are frightened and running out of
       daylight.

THE SOURCES DOUBLE AS SOUND EFFECTS. earth_box is already in the sound sheet
at two variants for exactly these chapters. Record the pool once and it
serves both the one-shots and the percussion, which is the cheapest file in
the whole production.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

### 6 · THE DEMETER      Chapter 7 only.     ← THE SECOND HORN
                          AND THE ONLY PERCUSSION IN THE PRODUCTION

THE BEAT IS THE SHIP'S ROUTINE. The log is dated entries — a watch kept, a
position noted, a bell rung, every day, while the crew goes one at a time. A
steady pulse is that routine, and the fact that it DOES NOT STOP while the
voices disappear is the whole horror of the chapter. Nothing else in the
production has percussion, and nothing else should.

KEEP IT SLOW. 60-70 BPM, which is near speech tempo rather than against it.
A faster pulse fights the reader's own rhythm and the listener stops hearing
words. Soft backbeat, nothing snapping.

Slow heavy 4/4 at 65 BPM, deep sub bass and a soft muffled kick, low-key and
restrained, modern cinematic electronic underscore, with INTRICATE SPARSE
PIANO over the top — a repeating figure, slightly off the grid, more and more
exposed as the track proceeds. Low string drone beneath. A sense of a vessel
under way at night. The arrangement THINS as it goes: layers leave and the
pulse remains. Near the end ONE DISTANT FRENCH HORN, a single held note, far
off and unanswered. No snare, no hi-hat, no build, no drop, no melody in the
mid range. 7 minutes

THE THINNING IS THE POINT and it matches the five bed states in the sound
sheet. Begin with bass, kick, piano, strings. End with the kick and almost
nothing else, still keeping time, nobody steering.

THE SECOND AND LAST HORN IN THE PRODUCTION. The first is the pursuit; this
one is earlier and quieter, and nobody will remember hearing it until the
horns return in chapter 26.

AVOID  snare, hi-hat, trap percussion, a drop, a build, anything that reads
       as a track rather than a score. It is a watch being kept, not a beat.



==============================================================================
ENVELOPES — NOTHING STARTS OR STOPS ABRUPTLY
==============================================================================

EVERY SOUND IN THIS PRODUCTION IS RAMPED. No gain is ever set by assignment,
no source is ever stopped at full level, and no bed is ever cut. Web Audio
does all of it natively on a GainNode, so this costs nothing but discipline.

THE ONE RULE THAT PREVENTS THE MOST DAMAGE: A GAIN CHANGE WITHOUT A RAMP IS
A CLICK. Starting or stopping a buffer at non-zero amplitude puts a step
discontinuity into the waveform and the listener hears a tick. Even 15 ms of
ramp removes it entirely. There is no case anywhere in this production for
setting gain instantly.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

FADE TIMES BY MATERIAL

  BED          in 4 s      out 6 s     crossfade 8 s
               Atmosphere should arrive before you notice it and leave after.
               A bed that fades in over 4 seconds is never heard starting.

  SCORE        in 6 s      out 8 s     crossfade 10 s
               Longer than the bed, always. If the score and the bed change
               at the same chapter boundary, the bed leads and the score
               follows — two staggered changes read as one smooth one.

  ONE-SHOT     in 0 s      out: its own tail
               NEVER FADE IN A TRANSIENT. A door, a timpani hit, a crate
               landing — the attack IS the sound. Fading one in destroys it
               and makes it sound like a sample being faded in, which is
               exactly what it is and exactly what must not be audible.
               They play at full on the first sample and decay naturally.

  DUCK         down 250 ms    up 1200 ms
               ASYMMETRIC, AND THAT IS THE POINT. Fast down so the bed is
               out of the way before the first syllable; slow back up so it
               returns underneath without anyone noticing it left. Equal
               times in both directions sounds like a machine breathing.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

CROSSFADES ARE EQUAL-POWER, NOT LINEAR

Two linear ramps crossing in the middle sum to about 70 per cent amplitude
at the midpoint, so every chapter transition dips audibly. Use the
equal-power curve — gain follows cos and sin of the progress rather than
the progress itself — and the sum stays constant across the whole fade.

    outgoing    gain = cos(t * PI/2)
    incoming    gain = sin(t * PI/2)

setValueCurveAtTime takes the array directly. A hundred points over the fade
is more than enough.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

THE EXPONENTIAL RAMP CANNOT REACH ZERO

exponentialRampToValueAtTime throws on a target of 0 and silently does
nothing useful near it. To fade a bed out and stop it:

    g.gain.exponentialRampToValueAtTime(0.0001, now + 6);
    g.gain.setValueAtTime(0, now + 6.01);
    src.stop(now + 6.02);

Exponential for fades down, because loudness is logarithmic and an
exponential fade is the one that sounds linear. Linear ramps for ducking,
where the move is small and speed matters more than smoothness.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

AT THE SEAMS

  CHAPTER TO CHAPTER   the outgoing bed and score begin their fades BEFORE
                       the last cue ends, not after. Eight seconds of overlap
                       under the final sentences, so the chapter ends into
                       the next rather than against it.

  BED STATE CHANGE     the Demeter and the castle progress through states.
                       These are crossfades between two mixes of the same
                       material, 10-12 s, and they should be imperceptible.
                       The listener must never catch the ship emptying.

  THE EXCEPTION        the Demeter landing, cue 6. One enormous timpani hit
                       and the pulse STOPS DEAD — no fade, no tail, nothing.
                       It is the only hard cut in the production and it only
                       works because everything else is ramped.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

SILENCE IS SCORED TOO. Chapter 12 has no cue, but the chapter before it must
still fade OUT into that silence over eight seconds, and the chapter after
must fade UP out of it. An abrupt arrival at silence reads as a fault; an
approach to it reads as a decision.

==============================================================================
PERCUSSION DOCTRINE — A CLOSED SET OF FOUR
==============================================================================

THE TEMPTATION IS TO USE DRUMS EVERYWHERE AND THAT IS THE ARGUMENT AGAINST IT.
Drums do pacing better than anything else available, which is exactly why they
stop working once they are always there. Twenty-seven chapters of piano and
strings are what make a kettle drum in chapter 27 mean something. The list
below is CLOSED. If a fifth place seems to want drums, the answer is no.

TWO INSTRUMENTS, TWO JOBS, AND THEY DO NOT SWAP.

  TIMPANI   weight and dread. They do not keep time, they ARRIVE. Used where
            something enormous is happening to people who cannot stop it.
  SNARE     military and motion. They DO keep time. Used where men are
            marching, riding, or travelling with purpose.

If a scene is neither martial nor moving, neither instrument has a reason to
be in it. That single test is what keeps the set closed.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1 · THE DEMETER — THE STORM AND THE LANDING          ch 7, final entries
    TIMPANI, heavy.

    The slow 65 BPM pulse has been keeping the ship's routine for the whole
    chapter. At the storm it is JOINED, not replaced: timpani rolls under the
    existing kick, swelling and receding with the weather, no fixed pattern.
    At the landing — the ship driving onto the shingle at Whitby with a dead
    man lashed to the wheel — one enormous timpani hit and then NOTHING. The
    pulse that has run for seven minutes stops dead.

    THAT SILENCE IS THE LOUDEST MOMENT IN THE PRODUCTION. Everything else in
    the percussion doctrine exists to make it possible.

2 · THE MARTIAL HISTORY                              ch 3
    SNARE, military, distant.

    The Count on the Szekely blood, Attila, and the wars against the Turk.
    It is the only place in the book where he stops being courteous and
    becomes a soldier boasting, mid-sentence, in front of a guest.

    A distant military snare, dry and old, more like a memory of a drum than
    a drum. It enters UNDER HIS SPEECH and leaves when he does. The dread cue
    continues underneath throughout — the snare is a layer, not a cue.

    THE ONLY TIME THE COUNT IS SCORED. Everywhere else he gets the room and
    nothing else, which is why this works.

3 · TRAVEL AND MOTION                                ch 1, 20, 26
    SNARE, light, rhythmic.

    The calèche through the pass; the earth-boxes tracked across London; the
    chase east by rail and cart. A snare does rail and hoof rhythm better
    than any sound effect, and it does something a recording cannot: it
    tells the listener how long the journey is going to take.

    Light, brushed, well back. It sets pace and gets out of the way.

4 · THE CASTLE, THE RING OF FIRE                     ch 27
    TIMPANI AND SNARE TOGETHER, and the horns.

    The only place both are used at once, and the only full orchestral moment
    in the production. Snare driving the pursuit, timpani under the fight at
    the gates, horns over the top. Everything held back for twenty-six
    chapters spends itself here.

    AND IT STOPS BEFORE THE END. The Count goes to dust at sunset and the
    last page is quiet. Do not score the ending; score the arrival.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHERE DRUMS MUST NOT GO, however much they would work:
  Whitby, any chapter      — the sea is the rhythm there
  Seward's diary           — clinical means no pulse at all
  Lucy's death, ch 12      — the one chapter with no score of any kind
  The tomb, ch 15-16       — timpani would be obvious, and obvious is worse
  Harker in the castle, ch 2-4  — the point is that nothing is happening yet
